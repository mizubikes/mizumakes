The Prusa CORE One+ Up Close: A 55 °C Heated Chamber and What Else Sets It Apart

The Prusa CORE One+ is an enclosed CoreXY workhorse with an actively heated 55 °C chamber. Here's what the chamber unlocks, what the '+' added, and the features that actually matter — plus the honest trade-offs.
August 19, 2026 · 6 min · Jared

If you’ve been circling the Prusa CORE One+, the feature everyone asks about first is the heated chamber — and it’s the right thing to ask about, because it’s the single biggest reason to pick this machine over an open-frame printer. But it’s not the only thing worth knowing. Here’s a plain-English tour of what the CORE One+ actually is, what the chamber buys you, and the smaller features that add up to a nicer machine to live with.

First, a naming knot to untangle, because it trips up almost everyone: “CORE One+” and “CORE One Plus” are the same machine — the current standard model, not a pricier tier. It replaced the original 2024 CORE One rather than sitting above it, and existing CORE One owners can upgrade to “+” spec cheaply (print the parts from Printables, or buy the small official kit). More on that below. And as of a mid-August 2026 lineup refresh, there’s now a CORE One+ (Gen 2) with further tweaks — cleaner print starts, better surface finish, easier operation — so if you’re buying new, confirm which revision you’re looking at.

The heated chamber, and why it’s the headline#

The CORE One+ is a fully enclosed CoreXY with active chamber temperature control up to 55 °C. That number sounds modest, but it’s the difference between “PLA and PETG only” and a genuinely material-flexible printer.

An open printer struggles with ABS, ASA, PC, and nylon because those filaments warp and split as they cool unevenly — corners lift off the plate, layers crack. A warm, enclosed chamber keeps the whole part in a stable thermal environment while it prints, which is what makes those engineering-grade materials reliable instead of a gamble. In practice, 55 °C comfortably covers ABS and ASA, and opens the door to PC and nylon. (For the lower-temperature end and where each everyday material earns its place, see my PLA vs PETG breakdown.)

The “+” made the chamber smarter, too. It has an automatic top vent: the extruder opens the vent for low-temp materials like PLA and PETG — which actually want cooler air to keep detail crisp — and closes it for high-temp runs where you want the heat trapped. On the original CORE One you flipped that grille by hand and hoped you remembered. Now it’s genuinely set-and-forget when you switch between, say, a PLA figurine and an ABS bracket in the same week.

What the “+” added over the original CORE One#

Beyond the auto vent, the “+” revision is a bundle of quality-of-life upgrades:

  • Better filament loading, especially for flexibles. An improved feed path and an advanced side filament sensor with a hardware switch make TPU load and run without snagging — historically the fussiest thing to feed on any printer.
  • A redesigned bayonet spoolholder. It locks firmly so the spool doesn’t walk sideways during fast retractions, and it’s modular — swap in a wider version for 2 kg Prusament spools, or mount a drybox for moisture-sensitive filament.
  • All firmware improvements rolled in, and a free/cheap upgrade path so existing owners aren’t left behind. That “your machine keeps getting better” philosophy is core to how Prusa operates.

The other features that matter#

The chamber gets the headlines, but a few things underneath do the quiet work:

  • CoreXY speed. Prusa officially rates the CORE One as roughly 20% faster than the bed-slinger MK4S at equivalent quality, a margin outside reviewers have independently verified. It’s quick without needing you to trade away surface quality.
  • All-steel exoskeleton. The steel case is the frame, which keeps the machine rigid at speed and, per Prusa, very hard to damage. Nothing’s welded or glued — it comes apart with a screwdriver, which matters when something eventually needs service.
  • Nextruder with a high-flow CHT nozzle as standard, plus load-cell first-layer calibration that sets the gap automatically instead of asking you to eyeball it. The “+” Nextruder adds 360° part cooling for cleaner overhangs.
  • Quiet. Around 48 dB in normal operation — closer to “background hum” than “shop tool.”
  • The Prusa ecosystem in one box: PrusaSlicer, Prusa Connect for remote monitoring, a massive model library on Printables, open-source hardware and firmware, and long-term support. It’s an enclosed CoreXY that earned All3DP’s Best Enclosed 3D Printer honors and a Red Dot Award.

Room to grow: MMU3 and the INDX#

One reason the CORE One+ is a smart long-term buy is that multi-material isn’t a dead end. You can add the MMU3 for affordable five-filament color on a single nozzle, or step up to the INDX, the eight-tool changer that gives each material its own dedicated nozzle for true functional multi-material — mixed flexibility, soluble supports, different nozzle sizes in one print. I dug into whether that’s worth it right now in why the 8-tool Prusa INDX might be my next printer (short version: exciting, but mid-rollout).

How it compares to Bambu#

The obvious cross-shop is Bambu Lab, and the split comes down to philosophy. Bambu leans closed and appliance-like — a slick sealed AMS and a “just works” experience, which is exactly why I still point color-first buyers there in my P1S vs P2S guide and the 2026 Bambu lineup rundown. The CORE One+ trades a little of that turnkey polish for openness, repairability, and a multi-material path you own rather than rent. If you value being able to fix, mod, and understand your machine, that trade is the whole point.

The honest trade-offs#

  • It’s heavy. Around 22.5 kg. It’s not a printer you relocate casually — plan its shelf before you buy.
  • The interface feels dated. It’s a scroll-wheel-and-button UI rather than a modern touchscreen. It works fine; it just doesn’t feel 2026.
  • It’s a newer platform. The troubleshooting history is thinner than something that’s been in thousands of hands for years, and early kits weren’t drama-free. If you want the most well-trodden path, factor that in.

What it costs#

As listed on Prusa’s CORE One+ page in mid-2026, it starts around $949 for the kit or $1,199 assembled — verify the current figure and exactly what’s in the box before you order, since the mid-August Gen 2 refresh and ongoing pricing changes can move both. (Third-party sellers often list higher.)

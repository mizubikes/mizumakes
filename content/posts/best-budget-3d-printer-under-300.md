For most people, the best budget 3D printer under $300 is the Bambu Lab A1 mini, as long as your parts fit in a 180 mm cube and you stick to PLA, PETG, and TPU. If you need a bigger bed for $200 or less, the Creality Ender-3 V3 SE is the practical pick. If you need ASA, ABS, or carbon-filled nylon, you need an enclosure, and the only machine near this budget that has one is the Elegoo Centauri Carbon, when you can find it at its street price.

The reasoning matters more than the pick, so here is how I would choose for functional, load-bearing parts. Prices below were checked in the last few weeks and move constantly, so treat them as snapshots.

## What actually matters in a budget 3D printer for functional parts?

Three specs decide whether a cheap printer can make useful parts: the maximum bed temperature, whether the frame is enclosed, and what the nozzle is made of. Speed claims barely matter. Every machine here advertises a top speed it will never hold on a real part with corners.

Bed temperature and enclosure set your material ceiling. An open frame with a bed that tops out at 80 °C is a PLA, PETG, and TPU machine. Bambu's own spec sheet lists PLA, PETG, TPU and PVA as suitable and discourages ABS, ASA, PC, PA, PET and fiber-reinforced polymers on the A1 mini. That is a materials limit, not a quality complaint.

Nozzle material sets your filler tolerance. The A1 mini ships with a 0.4 mm stainless steel nozzle, and the Ender-3 V3 SE ships with a brass nozzle. Neither is meant for glass- or carbon-filled filament without a swap.

A direct-drive extruder is the fourth thing to insist on, because it handles TPU and makes PETG retraction far less fussy. Every printer below has one.

## Best budget 3D printer under $300 for small PLA and PETG parts

The [Bambu Lab A1 mini](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Bambu+Lab+A1+mini&tag=mizubikes-web-20) is the low-fuss answer. Bambu's US store listed it at $219, or $329 as a Combo with the AMS lite in mid-October. It is a 180 mm open-frame bed slinger that calibrates itself.

The 180 mm cube is the real constraint. That is about 5.8 L of volume, versus about 16.8 L on the full-size A1, according to the comparison table at Bambu Reviews. For brackets, clips, spacers, cable management, small enclosures, and jig components, that is plenty. For a 200 mm project box, it is not.

If you want the bigger bed and Bambu's workflow, the [Bambu Lab A1](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Bambu+Lab+A1+3D+printer&tag=mizubikes-web-20) was logged at $259 on October 27. It also gets a 100 °C bed, which gives PETG a bit more adhesion headroom. If you are weighing it against a more capable Bambu, my [P1S long-term review](/posts/bambu-p1s-review-1000-hours/) covers what an enclosure buys.

## Is the Ender-3 V3 SE still worth it?

Yes, if budget is the first constraint. The [Creality Ender-3 V3 SE](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Creality+Ender-3+V3+SE&tag=mizubikes-web-20) has a Sprite direct-drive extruder, CR Touch leveling, dual Z screws, and linear shafts on Y. Creality's store lists it at $199, and one retailer shows $179.

The tradeoffs are real. The hotend is PTFE-lined with a 260 °C ceiling, so PETG and TPU are fine but you should not push it toward ASA-range temperatures. It runs Marlin, with a recommended top speed around 180 mm/s. That is slower than the Bambu, but it is plenty for functional parts, where wall strength matters more than finishing quickly.

The [Ender-3 V3 KE](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Creality+Ender-3+V3+KE&tag=mizubikes-web-20) steps up to a 300 °C copper nozzle with a 60 W ceramic heater, an X-axis linear rail, and Klipper-based firmware. At the $279 list price it fits under the cap, but it is still open frame, so you gain temperature headroom without gaining ASA capability.

## What about the Elegoo Neptune 4 Pro and Centauri Carbon?

The [Elegoo Neptune 4 Pro](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Elegoo+Neptune+4+Pro&tag=mizubikes-web-20) is the spec-sheet value pick among open-frame machines. Elegoo's US store lists it at $239 with a 300 °C nozzle, 121-point auto leveling, and a 225 × 225 × 265 mm volume. It also has a segmented heated bed with a 100 W center zone and a 150 W outer zone, which saves heat-up time on small parts. Its listed material support includes ASA and nylon, but the frame is open. Treat that as marketing unless you build an enclosure.

The [Elegoo Centauri Carbon](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Elegoo+Centauri+Carbon&tag=mizubikes-web-20) is the one that changes the equation. It is a fully enclosed CoreXY with a cast aluminum frame, a hardened steel nozzle, a 320 °C hotend, and a 256 mm cube build volume. One tracker logged $299 in late September. Its limits are a passive chamber (no active heating) and an ecosystem that is less polished than Bambu's. One review notes it is not ideal for complete beginners. It is still the only route I found to ASA and CF-filled parts at roughly this budget.

For context on what an actively heated chamber does, see my write-up on the [Prusa CORE One+ chamber](/posts/prusa-core-one-plus-heated-chamber-features/). That is a different price class, but it shows what you give up with a passive enclosure.

## What can you actually print on a $200 printer?

TDS values are measured on ideal specimens, and printed parts land lower, especially across layers. On an open-frame budget printer, you are mostly choosing between PLA, PETG, and TPU, and PETG is the functional workhorse. My [PLA vs PETG comparison](/posts/pla-vs-petg/) covers the numbers, and the [PETG brand roundup](/posts/best-petg-filament-brands-functional-parts/) compares spools on TDS data.

Starting settings differ by printer. Bambu Studio's stock A1 mini PETG profile uses 250 °C nozzle, 70 °C textured bed, and 60% fan. One printer's profile will not transfer cleanly to another, so check your spool's TDS and adjust. On a PTFE-lined hotend like the Ender-3 V3 SE, stay comfortably below the 260 °C cap.

Dry your PETG. A damp roll is the single most common cause of a bad PETG print. My [filament drying guide](/posts/how-to-dry-filament-dryer-box-vs-dehydrator-vs-heated-bed/) compares the options.

On the design side, the weak axis is always layer adhesion. Orient parts so the load runs along the layers, not across them. For slicer settings, one community profile for the A1 mini suggests 3–4 walls and 20–40% gyroid infill for PETG. Walls carry more load than infill, so add walls before you add infill. Use heat-set inserts rather than threading plastic for any fastener you will tighten more than a few times.

## When not to buy a budget printer

Skip the open-frame machines if your parts will sit in a hot car, near a motor, or outdoors in direct sun. PLA and PETG will creep or soften under sustained load and heat. That is the job for ASA, covered in [what is ASA filament](/posts/what-is-asa-filament/), and it needs an enclosure. A tent around an open printer is a workaround, not a spec.

Skip the A1 mini if you regularly need parts over 180 mm. Skip the Ender-3 V3 SE if you want Wi-Fi monitoring, since it has an SD-card and USB-C workflow with no wireless.

The $300 cap also leaves little for extras. Budget for a dryer, a hardened nozzle if you plan on fillers, and spare build plates.

## So which one should you buy?

For small functional PLA and PETG parts with minimal setup, buy the A1 mini when it is at or near $219. For a larger bed at the lowest price, buy the Ender-3 V3 SE. For ASA or filled materials, hunt for the Centauri Carbon near $300, and if it is not available, save for a proper enclosed machine rather than modifying an open-frame one.

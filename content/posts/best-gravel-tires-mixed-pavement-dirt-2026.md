A tire is a materials-and-tolerances problem like any other. You're trading rolling resistance against grip, and casing weight against puncture protection. Mixed pavement and dirt is the hardest version of that trade, because the tire has to be decent at two jobs that pull in opposite directions.

I haven't ridden any of these tires for this post. What follows is a read of the 2026 group tests and lab data, plus a way to decide between them. Where the sources disagree, I'll say so.

## What mixed-surface riding asks of a tire

Most gravel rides are a blend: a paved connector, a hardpack road, and maybe a loose or washboarded stretch. A knobby tread buys grip on the loose parts. On pavement you pay for it with extra watts and some noise. A tight, low-profile tread does the reverse.

The gap between tires is bigger than most people assume. In Gran Fondo's 32-tire test, <cite index="19-3">the most and least efficient tires differed by more than 11 watts of rolling resistance</cite>. Their rig <cite index="19-5">measured at 20 km/h with each tire loaded to 50 kg</cite>. Across a pair of tires that works out to more than 22 watts. That's the kind of difference you'd feel over a long day.

One caution before you compare numbers. Different labs use different drums, loads, and pressures. Gran Fondo's 45 mm figures and Bicycle Rolling Resistance's 40 mm figures aren't interchangeable. Compare within one test, not across them.

## The all-rounders: where most people should start

**Schwalbe G-One RX Pro.** This was Gran Fondo's overall winner. They described it as <cite index="13-7">surprisingly fast, with loads of grip, good compliance and outstanding puncture protection</cite>. It's also the pick for muddy conditions in Velo's guide. If you want one tire for everything and don't want to think about it, this is the one the data supports most strongly. [Schwalbe G-One RX Pro on Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Schwalbe+G-One+RX+Pro&tag=mizubikes-web-20)

**Schwalbe G-One Overland.** Cycling Weekly still ranks it as its best all-rounder. The reasons it gives are <cite index="3-4">low rolling resistance, weight, puncture protection and value, now with a 50mm version</cite>. It's a good fit if you'd rather not pay premium-race-tire money. [Schwalbe G-One Overland on Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Schwalbe+G-One+Overland&tag=mizubikes-web-20)

**Maxxis Rambler.** Gran Fondo named it Best Buy, citing <cite index="13-9">impressively high grip and reassuring handling</cite>. The tested tire weighed <cite index="38-4">557 g, right in the middle of the field</cite>. The tradeoff is pavement manners. Cycling Weekly's older review called it slow on pavement, and Bike Perfect described the large-volume version as <cite index="43-3">balanced for most conditions short of proper mud</cite>. Maxxis sells many Rambler variants, and the lab says other versions may perform differently, so check the casing before you buy. [Maxxis Rambler on Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Maxxis+Rambler+gravel+tire&tag=mizubikes-web-20)

**Goodyear Peak Tubeless Complete.** Cycling Weekly picked it for mixed terrain, noting <cite index="23-9">low rolling resistance for its grip</cite> and easy installation. It tends to get less attention than the Schwalbes, so it's worth a look if you're price-shopping. [Goodyear Peak Tubeless Complete on Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Goodyear+Peak+Tubeless+Complete+gravel+tire&tag=mizubikes-web-20)

## If pavement is half your ride

The fastest options give up some grip on loose ground, and for a lot of riders that's the right call.

**Schwalbe G-One RS Pro** led Gran Fondo's efficiency chart at <cite index="13-5">10.6 W, followed by the Continental Terra Competition at 11.1 W</cite>. Velo makes it their best overall pick, pointing to the combination of speed and tackiness. BikeRadar's testers found it especially good on dry and hardpack terrain. Velo does note that some riders will balk at the weight. [Schwalbe G-One RS Pro on Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Schwalbe+G-One+RS+Pro&tag=mizubikes-web-20)

**Hutchinson Caracal Race** is Velo's best all-road tire. It <cite index="25-6">sits at the top of the rolling-resistance leaderboards Velo cites</cite>. Cycling Weekly rates it as its fast-gravel choice. Treat it as a road-leaning tire with just enough traction for dry gravel. [Hutchinson Caracal Race on Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Hutchinson+Caracal+Race&tag=mizubikes-web-20)

**Continental Terra Speed and Terra Competition** are the other fast-rolling picks. In Gran Fondo's table the 45 mm Terra Speed measured 12.5 W at 530 g. Its pinch-flat protection score was just 1 on their scale, so tubeless and sensible pressures matter with it. [Continental Terra Speed on Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Continental+Terra+Speed+gravel+tire&tag=mizubikes-web-20)

**Pirelli Cinturato Gravel H** suits hardpack and pavement-heavy routes. The 2026 SmartEvo version scored 17.9 W in the Bicycle Rolling Resistance lab at 40 mm. That site called it <cite index="21-10">balanced, with better puncture resistance and outstanding wet grip on hard surfaces</cite>. [Pirelli Cinturato Gravel H on Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Pirelli+Cinturato+Gravel+H&tag=mizubikes-web-20)

## If dirt is the priority

When your "mixed" ride is closer to 70% dirt, a grippier tread makes sense.

BikeRadar's pick for mixed conditions is the **Pirelli Cinturato Gravel RC**. They say it <cite index="10-3">excels on most surfaces, with great grip, durability and a dirt-shedding tread</cite>, though it isn't ideal for deep mud. Its sibling, the **Cinturato Gravel M**, is stickier still. BikeRadar lists it at <cite index="10-5">$77 and praises the compound and puncture resistance</cite>, and warns that it can be tricky to seat. Flow Mountain Bike found it came into its own on looser ground but was a bit slow on tarmac and hardpack. [Pirelli Cinturato Gravel RC on Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Pirelli+Cinturato+Gravel+RC&tag=mizubikes-web-20)

If your bike has the clearance, Velo's mountain-bike-tire-for-gravel pick is the **Vittoria Peyote**. Velo says it combines <cite index="25-11">exceptional traction on loose-over-hard surfaces with some of the best pavement rolling resistance for a mountain bike tread</cite>. [Vittoria Peyote on Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Vittoria+Peyote+gravel+tire&tag=mizubikes-web-20)

One caveat on tight-tread tires like the Panaracer GravelKing SK. Cycling Weekly liked its grip and low rolling resistance on harder surfaces, but said the tread <cite index="23-12">fills up quickly and doesn't shed mud fast</cite>. If your dirt turns to clay, that matters.

## Pressure and setup matter as much as the model

Tubeless is the default for gravel now. One guide says <cite index="31-5">tubeless usually lets you run 5 to 8 psi lower than tubes without extra flat risk</cite>. Pressure advice varies a lot, though. One guide suggests <cite index="31-3">30–40 psi for 40mm tires and 25–35 psi for 45mm</cite>. A calculator I found gives about 40 psi front and 44 psi rear for a 75 kg rider on 40 mm tubeless. Rim width, rider weight, and surface all move the number.

Treat any chart as a starting point. Adjust in small steps and note how the bike feels on both pavement and dirt. A common tip is to <cite index="31-5">run the front a couple of psi lower than the rear</cite>. Also check your rim's pressure rating. Hookless rims have limits, and narrow tires on them are the situation where people tend to run into trouble.

Sizing deserves a second look too. Some tires measure narrower than their label on certain rims. Cycling Weekly noted that its 38 mm Rambler measured exactly 38 mm on a wide rim and could come in smaller on a standard one. Check your frame clearance against the measured width, not the printed number.

## How I'd choose

For a true 50/50 rider, I'd start with the G-One RX Pro or the Overland, based on the testing above. For a budget-leaning grippy option, the Maxxis Rambler is the one the testers recommend. If you ride mostly paved or hardpack and want speed, go with the RS Pro or Caracal Race. If your routes are rough and loose, the Pirelli RC or M is the better fit.

None of these is a bad choice. The bigger mistake is buying a race-oriented tire for a ride that's mostly loose dirt, or a mud-ready tread for a ride that's mostly pavement.
